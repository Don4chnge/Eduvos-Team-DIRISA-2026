One-party dominance has collapsed

Share of municipalities in each competition regime.

Hung councils after each election

Leading party below 50% of the PR vote. 2021 more than doubled the previous high.

In 2000, most municipalities were governed by one party with a comfortable majority. Over five elections that picture changed: the typical council went from about 1.8 effective parties to about 2.6, and the leading party's median vote share fell from 70% to 55%.

We first tested whether voter turnout rises and falls with this competition, as an inverted U. It did not hold in any election or within municipalities over time. The clearest consequence of fragmentation is instead the rise of hung councils, which is what this dashboard forecasts.

Why a simple model beat XGBoost

Each training example pairs a municipality at one election with whether its council was hung at the next. We trained on 2000 to 2016 and kept 2021 hidden as the final test.

Standard cross-validation favoured XGBoost, but it mixes elections, so the model can learn from the future to predict the past. When we validated in time order, the patterns XGBoost learned in one era did not carry into the next. A logistic regression using only the leading party's current vote share forecast future elections best, and it was chosen before we looked at the 2021 test.

PR-AUC measures how well each model finds the rarer hung councils. Validation: 2011 and 2016 elections, trained only on earlier ones.

From raw results to a 2026 forecast

  1. CollectOfficial IEC results for the 2000, 2006, 2011, 2016 and 2021 local government elections, from the voting-district level upward.
  2. CleanRemoved parks and nature reserves, fixed impossible turnout values, harmonised party names, combined the nine 2021 provincial files in code so none were lost, and proved that the 2016 turnout column counted only the leading party's votes. Every fix is recorded in a cleaning log.
  3. LinkFollowed 263 municipalities across 21 years of boundary and code changes, so each council has a history.
  4. Measure competitionEffective Number of Parties, leading party's share and winning margin, all from the PR ballot.
  5. Test the first ideaAn inverted U between competition and turnout was tested with the Lind–Mehlum test and fixed-effects models, and rejected.
  6. Model hung councilsKMeans describes competition regimes; four forecasting models were compared with grouped and forward-chaining cross-validation.
  7. Forecast 2026The chosen model was retrained on all elections and applied to each municipality's 2021 result, with swing scenarios on top.
  8. Simulate election dayA turnout gap between the leading party's supporters and other voters is converted into a vote-share change: new share = s(1+t) ÷ (s(1+t) + 1 − s). The points swing is added, and the result goes through the same model.

Sources

  • Electoral Commission of South Africa (IEC), municipal election results 2000–2021: results.elections.org.za
  • Municipal Demarcation Board, local municipality and province boundaries
  • Laakso and Taagepera (1979), Effective Number of Parties; Lind and Mehlum (2010), U-shape test; Chen and Guestrin (2016), XGBoost

What to keep in mind

  • A council counts as hung when the leading party wins less than 50% of the PR vote. Seat rounding can occasionally differ from this.
  • The model learns from past elections only. Polls, new parties and local events are not included, which is why the swing scenarios matter.
  • The cleaned 2016 turnout column counted only the leading party's votes, so 2016 turnout was excluded; 2016 vote shares are used.
  • History for merged municipalities follows the predecessor that could be matched by name or code.
